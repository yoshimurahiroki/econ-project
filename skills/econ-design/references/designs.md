## Model construction

Start with the decision structure, primitives and requested object. Add the branches required by the model's information, strategic interaction, contracts or continuation choices. Use the solution and comparative-static branches for the object being derived.

1. **Choose the decision structure.** For a price-taking actor, write the feasible set and objective conditional on prices. When payoffs depend on others' choices, specify players, actions and the strategic solution concept. Derive contingent strategies from timing and information sets: a follower's strategy specifies actions after possible histories. Private types require conditional beliefs and type-dependent strategies. A designer choosing payments or rules needs the contract branch below; actions that change future opportunities need the dynamic branch.

2. **Type primitives and check compatible assumptions.** Define spaces and domains before objects on them: a commodity dimension and consumption set before preferences and endowments, or a type space before beliefs and strategies. Separate feasible actions from exogenous parameters. When introducing or combining assumptions leaves their joint feasibility unresolved, establish it by direct derivation or an admissible example. Use boundary examples when an illustration may import an additional restriction. Ordinal rankings suffice for ranking choices; use numerical utility with the representation and normalization needed for derivatives, risk or cardinal welfare operations. Establish the extra comparability assumptions before aggregating utility across people.

3. **Place shocks in information and time.** For each consequential shock, specify who observes it and when relative to choices. Under expected-utility or expected-payoff maximization, integrate unresolved payoff uncertainty conditional on the actor's information. For a shock known to the actor but unobserved by the researcher, solve the actor's problem conditional on its realization, then integrate over it in the observation mapping. Derive observable probabilities or moments from this timing, distinguishing private information, choice error and measurement error. Changing the observation time can change the optimization problem and the endogeneity of the resulting estimating equation.

4. **Close individual decisions with equilibrium conditions.** Separate each actor's optimization from consistency of choices, beliefs and aggregates. Add the market-clearing, resource, mobility, budget or strategic consistency conditions needed for the endogenous quantities. In an open-city closure, outside utility is fixed and population adjusts; in a closed-city closure, population is fixed and utility adjusts. A policy fixing a formerly endogenous boundary or quantity can replace its free-boundary condition: a binding development limit can generate a boundary rent above the outside land rent. Derive which condition changes before solving the new system. Establish whether the solution set is empty, single or multiple; use a justified selection rule or retain set-valued predictions when multiplicity matters.

5. **Establish optimizing actions.** For discrete actions, compare feasible payoffs directly. For a calculus solution, examine boundaries and active constraints; when using KKT, retain complementary slackness and check the constraint qualifications needed for necessary conditions. Use curvature, derivative signs or a direct comparison to establish global sufficiency. For maximization on [0,1], a differentiable objective can attain its maximum at 0 with f′(0) ≤ 0, at 1 with f′(1) ≥ 0, or at an interior stationary point; compare the feasible candidates. Establish existence, optimality and any uniqueness claim under their respective assumptions. When a numerical solution is requested, establish its convergence criterion, substitute the returned choices into the original constraints and optimality conditions, and compare their residuals with the required tolerance.

6. **Construct implementable contracts.** When actions or types are unobservable, condition allocations and transfers on contractible information. Write incentive compatibility against feasible deviations, individual rationality against the outside option, and transfer or resource restrictions separately. For a risk-neutral worker, let hidden effort cost e and raise success probability from 0 to p, with fixed pay w and success bonus b. Effort yields w + pb − e versus w from shirking, so IC gives pb ≥ e: the common fixed payment cancels. If p > 0, this gives b ≥ e/p; participation still compares w + pb − e with the outside option, while limited liability separately restricts payments. Check which constraints imply others, which bind, and how ties are resolved. Use a truthful-direct-mechanism reduction only under the information, commitment and equilibrium assumptions that support it.

7. **Construct a dynamic choice only when continuation matters.** Specify state s, control a, feasible actions A(s), transition s′ = T(s,a,ε′), flow payoff u, discount β, horizon and initial condition. Retain a state sufficient for payoff-relevant history. Under a valid stationary recursive representation, V(s) = max over a ∈ A(s) of {u(s,a) + β E[V(s′) | s,a]}; the policy g(s) selects actions while V(s) measures optimized continuation value. For a finite horizon, use time-indexed values and a terminal condition; for an infinite-horizon path, impose the appropriate transversality and admissibility conditions. Check the equivalence and regularity supporting the recursive or Euler characterization, and return the path, policy or value the question asks for.

8. **Reduce a system while preserving feasible choices and optimality.** When interaction operates through an aggregate, check whether payoffs and best responses can be represented using that aggregate. A reversible change of variables must preserve feasible domains and allow recovery of individual choices. Derive the aggregate consistency equation, recover individual choices from its solutions, and substitute those choices into the original individual optimality conditions. Where continuity, endpoint behavior and monotonicity establish existence or uniqueness, show that argument. Retain the disaggregated system when the proposed aggregate loses a relevant interaction or admits spurious solutions.

9. **Differentiate the requested object.** Distinguish a chosen action, an equilibrium outcome and optimized value. For a smooth local equilibrium F(y,z) = 0 with nonsingular F_y, derive dy/dz = −F_y⁻¹F_z. For optimized value, apply an envelope result under its differentiability and constraint conditions, including direct effects through changing constraints. A unique optimizer alone does not establish value differentiability. Resolve changing active sets, thresholds, kinks or equilibrium selection before extending a local derivative across regimes; give the sign or ranking on the domain where its conditions hold.

10. **Recheck results after changing behavior or institutions.** Identify the changed primitive, preference domain, permitted deviation or information set and rewrite the affected solution conditions. Check the assumptions supporting any existence, uniqueness, incentive or comparative-static result before carrying it over. For example, allowing couples to rank pairs of jobs changes both preferences and blocking deviations in a matching model. Preserve the benchmark to locate the source of a changed result, and re-examine fixed conditions that adjust when scale or setting changes.

### Ordered choices and constructive representations

- **Compare ordered choices through payoff differences.** For discrete, corner or nonsmooth choices, specify the choice and parameter orders and form ΔU(θ) = U(x_H,θ) − U(x_L,θ) for x_H > x_L. Single crossing preserves both weak and strict preference for the higher choice as θ rises. For vector choices, use quasi-supermodularity on a lattice and single crossing; supermodularity and increasing differences suffice. Order changing feasible sublattices by the strong set order: A ≥ B means a ∨ b ∈ A and a ∧ b ∈ B for every a ∈ A, b ∈ B. Compare the nonempty argmax sets in that order and their least/greatest elements when present; arbitrary selections through ties can fall. With an unresolved scalar state s, integrable single-crossing differences Δu(s) retain single crossing in their expectation under a monotone-likelihood-ratio shift, such as log-supermodular densities f(s;θ). First-order stochastic dominance suffices when Δu(s) is nondecreasing. For adoption paying s − c against zero, this stronger payoff shape permits the weaker distribution order.

- **Choose coordinates that preserve and expose the interaction.** For two substitutable choices, reversing one coordinate can turn their cross effect into complementarity; translate the resulting ordering back to the original choice. In larger systems, derive the signs after transformation. When the objective is f(x) + Σ_i g_i(x_i,y_i), independent local domains let maximization over y reduce it to f(x) + Σ_i max_y_i g_i(x_i,y_i). If f is supermodular, the reduced objective remains so because each added term depends on one x coordinate. Shared local constraints or cross-unit terms require a different reduction. Recover optimizing y_i from each reduced solution; use suprema and address attainment when maxima do not exist.

- **Construct the equilibrium correspondence.** Define each full best-response set B_i(x_−i), then B(x) = ∏_i B_i(x_−i). On a nonempty compact convex product strategy domain in finite-dimensional Euclidean space, continuous payoffs and quasi-concavity in own action give the nonempty compact convex values and upper hemicontinuity needed for a Kakutani fixed point. For opponent-dependent feasibility, derive these properties from the feasible correspondence. Recover simultaneous optimality from x ∈ B(x). Discrete actions sometimes admit a different convex representation: with ordered finite actions, compact interval private types, bounded atomless joint type density, finite payoff integrals and conditional-payoff single crossing whenever opponents use monotone strategies, represent monotone strategies by ordered cutoffs. Equal cutoffs allow skipped actions. Establish convex best responses and a closed graph in cutoff coordinates, then recover pure strategies and optimal boundary-type actions. Convex combinations of action levels require their own economic feasibility; a fixed point establishes existence, while uniqueness and iterative convergence need separate arguments.

### Information choices and belief representations

- **Construct disclosure through implementable beliefs.** When the intervention changes what receivers learn, specify state ω, prior μ₀, feasible signal kernels π(s|ω), receiver utility u and designer utility v. Under credible commitment to the known kernel, derive μ_s(ω) ∝ π(s|ω)μ₀(ω), receiver choices a(μ_s) ∈ argmax_a E_μ_s[u(a,ω)], and the designer's expected payoff, including tie behavior. Unrestricted experiments with a common prior permit choosing a distribution τ over full posteriors subject to E_τ[μ] = μ₀. For finite states and posterior support, recover π(s_μ|ω) = τ(μ)μ(ω)/μ₀(ω) on positive-prior states. With prior P(H)=1/4 and receiver threshold P(H|s)≥1/2, equal weights on posteriors 0 and 1/2 implement action at probability 1/2: the favorable signal occurs with probability 1 in H and 1/3 in L. Restricted experiments add feasibility conditions. Even with unrestricted experiments, preserving only the mean of posterior means generally does not ensure implementability. State-contingent messages chosen without commitment require a communication model supporting that behavior.

- **Make acquisition and remembered information endogenous when they affect choice.** Choose the feasible experiment menu and cost C(π) from the observation technology: paying for a specified test and choosing any signal kernel imply different attainable beliefs. Compare posterior decision value less cost and reoptimize acquisition when payoffs change. Shannon cost λI(ω;s) is one specification. For unrestricted experiments, finite actions and λ>0, its discrete-choice form P(i|ω) ∝ P(i)exp(u(i,ω)/λ) includes endogenous shares P(i) = E[P(i|ω)] and optimality inequalities for excluded alternatives; these shares can change with the prior or menu. When information persists, add the sufficient inherited belief to the physical state, update it after the chosen signal, and transition that belief into the next decision. A conditional-information cost prices new information given the history. Mean/variance compression requires a supporting family such as Gaussian beliefs; general learning may require the full posterior.

- **Select the uncertainty aggregator from the preference comparison.** For known-probability lotteries, expected utility and utility curvature describe risk attitudes under the relevant representation axioms. For state-contingent acts, distinguish a single subjective prior from ambiguity preferences; maxmin E_p[u] over p ∈ P requires its own preference restrictions. Researcher uncertainty about an estimate does not select ambiguity preferences. In an urn with known red share 1/3 and unknown blue/green split, preferring red to blue but blue-or-green to red-or-green implies contradictory rankings of red and blue under any single additive prior and common increasing money utility. Utility curvature alone cannot generate those rankings. For sequential maxmin, specify the information partition and updating rule. To represent ex ante maxmin recursively across acts under the specified information structure, require rectangularity: admissible event probabilities and conditional priors can be recombined within P. With nonrectangular P, distinguish ex ante commitment from conditional reoptimization; rectangularizing P changes the preference model.

## Choose the estimand and method

Reuse a settled specification. For a new analysis, establish the following chain in the existing specification or analysis code, recording only choices that determine the requested result.

1. **Requested use.** Choose the output: a descriptive quantity, prediction, causal effect, structural parameter, counterfactual outcome or action. Define the loss, welfare or profit comparison when the output selects an action.
2. **Target.** Write the parameter or contrast, its population, period and units. Specify average effect versus effect on treated units, conditional versus population average, level difference versus relative change, and static versus dynamic response as applicable. Set aggregation weights from that target.
3. **Observed variation.** Map records to units, panels, repeated cross sections, choice occasions and markets. Identify assignment, exposure, treatment histories, endogenous variables and the information available before choices. Preserve the eligibility and timing needed for the comparison.
4. **Model and identification.** Write the conditional mean, moment restriction, likelihood or equilibrium conditions that identify the target. Select controls, instruments and fixed effects from those conditions. Derive the remaining comparison after conditioning and the behavioral margin it measures.
5. **Estimator and inference.** Choose the estimator implementing that target on those observations. Match standard errors and resampling to assignment and dependence. State the actual subgroup or policy contrast before selecting its inference procedure.
6. **Execution.** Pass these choices to [the specialist implementation selector](../../econ-data/references/implementation.md#estimator-engine-and-algorithm-selection). Obtain the fitted sample, named estimates and required contrasts from the returned object, then calculate the quantity that answers the economic question.

Use the applicable row to narrow the method; the target and identification conditions choose among its implementations.

| Requested object and observed structure | Method and determining condition |
| --- | --- |
| A level association or additive conditional mean with covariates and fixed effects | OLS or linear fixed effects; interpret the coefficient as the defined projection or conditional contrast. Causal interpretation follows the assignment conditions. |
| A nonnegative conditional mean with proportional responses | Log-link Poisson quasi-likelihood with design-matched covariance; use the [scale branch](#outcome-scale-and-conditional-mean). |
| Count probabilities, dispersion or a specified count likelihood | Poisson, negative binomial or a specified two-part model according to the conditional distribution and participation mechanism. |
| Binary or multinomial choices | Logit, probit or random-coefficient choice models from the choice set and utility restrictions; calculate the requested probability or average marginal effect. |
| An endogenous treatment with an excluded instrument | IV or GMM under instrument relevance, exclusion and the conditions defining the compliance population or structural moment. |
| Treatment timing, eligibility and untreated trends | The [DID branch](#difference-in-differences-and-event-studies) selects common-adoption, cohort-time or treatment-path effects. |
| Assignment at a running-variable cutoff | Local polynomial RDD or the adopted local-randomization design; retain the local target and assignment rule. |
| A treated aggregate unit and a donor panel | Synthetic control, synthetic DID or interactive-effects methods under their respective weighting or counterfactual structure. |
| Flexible nuisance functions with an identified causal score | Double/debiased machine learning; choose the score for the target before the learner. |
| Conditional treatment effects used for targeting | Causal forests or another identified heterogeneous-effect estimator; use the corresponding target-population aggregation for the decision. |
| Demand substitution, markups or competitive counterfactuals | Structural demand and supply with identified price responses, ownership and conduct. |
| Entry, investment or replacement with continuation value | A dynamic choice or game model with state transitions, payoff normalization and an identified parameter vector. |

Evaluate a method change by the economic target or action it changes. Finish method selection once an eligible implementation supplies the required quantities within the task's resources. Preserve the adopted method when a proposed alternative changes only an irrelevant numerical detail.

## Outcome scale and conditional mean

Select the outcome model from the requested effect and conditional mean. A count with observed zeros remains admissible in a level OLS regression. With a correctly specified additive conditional mean, its slope gives a level change; under a projection interpretation, retain the projection's population and weighting. For a positive reference mean m, report a specified level contrast Δ as 100Δ/m percent of that reference. An individual growth rate 100(y1−y0)/y0 requires y0>0 and defines a different target. A mean of individual rates and a ratio of population means use different weights. Preserve the chosen denominator and calculate it on the defined population.

For μ(x)=E[Y|X=x]=exp(x′β), use Poisson quasi-likelihood to estimate proportional mean responses with zeros included. A binary-regressor change gives 100[exp(β)−1] percent at fixed remaining regressors; a unit change in a continuous regressor has the same finite-change expression, while β is the semi-elasticity. Aggregate fitted counterfactual means over the target population when interactions or heterogeneous slopes make the requested effect differ across observations. The conditional mean and design-matched covariance determine this use of PPML for both integer and noninteger nonnegative outcomes. Adding a constant before taking logarithms changes the target and its response to measurement units.

For a negative binomial NB2 model, write Var(Y|X)=μ+αμ² and the chosen link. Use its likelihood when the requested distribution or maintained mean–variance structure calls for it. A marginal variance larger than the marginal mean does not select NB2: heterogeneous conditional means also produce that pattern. Specify how individual effects enter. `MASS::glm.nb(y ~ x + factor(id))` estimates group intercepts by unconditional maximum likelihood; a panel command labelled conditional negative binomial imposes its own heterogeneity model. Establish the estimator's fixed-effect argument for the panel's time dimension. For binary-response fixed effects, likewise select conditional likelihood, bias correction or a sufficiently informative time dimension according to the adopted target.

An exposure e enters a log-link rate model as log(e) with coefficient fixed at one when the mean is e·exp(x′β). Preserve the exposure's units and positive domain. A share outcome has its stated denominator and support; select a linear share contrast, fractional-response mean or a binomial likelihood from the actual observation process. A separate zero/participation equation requires a corresponding behavioral or measurement process. For nonlinear models with absorbed effects, retain the package's dropped observations and separation handling as part of the fitted sample.

## Design elements

Define the comparison's treatment path or exposure mapping, potential outcomes, population, period and target parameter. Distinguish source-observation, analysis, policy-assignment and independent inference units from their roles in the actual design; preserve source labels without assigning undocumented roles. Explain identifying variation, risk set, assignment and counterfactual through institutions, timing and observed operations. Connect the maintained assumptions to the estimator, aggregation weights and inference unit.

Trace a causal threat through its common cause, timing, effect on treatment or selection, and effect on the outcome. Select controls from the causal structure and measurement timing. Explain the variation removed by a fixed-effect cell and the comparison that remains after conditioning. Predetermined measurement supplies temporal ordering. Compare the recorded assignment process with the stated exogeneity assumption.

For a diagnostic, specify the suspected alternative path, an outcome or proxy it predicts, and the conditioning that preserves the design's intended variation. Interpret rejection through the assumption or specification that this comparison tests, using the statistic and its uncertainty. State identifying assumptions as conditions linking the estimator to the parameter. Identify each reported result as an observation or estimate. Name the resulting object: association, prediction, causal effect, causal mediation or model counterfactual.

When the question requires comparing different decision-makers on the same case, look for a setting with multiple judgments of that case to test rankings, and use decision order or separate decisions to assess interaction among judges.

## Difference-in-differences and event studies

Define adoption dates, reversals, treatment intensity, anticipation and comparison eligibility before selecting an estimator. State the target in potential-outcome notation and the scale on which untreated trends are parallel. Use these branches:

- **One treated cohort and one pre/post contrast.** For a panel ATT under conditional parallel trends, choose an outcome-regression, inverse-weighted or doubly robust two-period estimator according to the adopted nuisance models. `DRDID` supplies distinct panel and repeated-cross-section estimators. A multi-year pre/post average is a specified outcome aggregation with its own period weights. A common-date linear fixed-effects comparison remains appropriate when its weighting and conditional mean implement the adopted target.
- **Staggered absorbing binary treatment.** Define ATT(g,t) for cohort g at time t and select never-treated or eligible not-yet-treated comparisons. Use a group-time estimator such as `did::att_gt` and aggregate with the target's cohort/event-time weights. For a cohort-interacted event study such as Sun–Abraham, retain its comparison cohort and aggregation. A static or conventional lead/lag TWFE coefficient mixes cohort-time effects and can use already-treated comparisons; derive its weights before adopting it as the target effect.
- **Treatment reversals or changing doses.** Specify the effect of the observed treatment path relative to its baseline path. Use a method supporting those histories, such as `DIDmultiplegtDYN::did_multiplegt_dyn`, with eligible groups sharing the baseline dose. Its non-normalized effect averages effects of the realized path relative to the baseline dose; its normalized effect is a weighted average of current and lagged dose effects. Preserve that distinction when reporting a per-unit effect, selecting horizons or comparing estimates.
- **Continuous treatment contrasts.** Define whether the target compares a dose with zero, compares two doses or measures a marginal dose response. Set the corresponding selection and trend conditions. Coding the dose as an adoption indicator changes the intervention and target.

For each event study, specify the reference period, window, eligible comparisons and contributing cohort or switcher population at each horizon. Fix the population across horizons when the requested dynamic comparison requires it. Match simultaneous or pointwise intervals to the reported family of effects. Preserve anticipation, spillover, composition and concurrent-policy conditions that determine the actual comparison.

Choose covariates from their causal role and timing. In a balanced-panel `did::att_gt` comparison, time-varying covariates use the earlier period; the unbalanced-panel/repeated-cross-section path uses period-specific covariates. Preserve the adopted interpretation when changing that option. `DRDID::drdid_panel` supplies one covariate matrix to both propensity-score and outcome models. Separate nuisance covariate sets require an implementation whose score and inference support that contract. A pooled TWFE regression with the same variable names does not reproduce the DR-DID ATT. An independently specified state-by-year adjustment must retain its measurement timing and identifying interpretation in each estimator.

For matching, specify pre-treatment variables, measurement dates, common support, matching weights and the subsequent counterfactual-trend assumption. For future-treated comparisons, specify the eligible pre-treatment window and event-timing assumptions. Map age, birth cohort, calendar time and event time to their roles in the design.

Interpret pre-treatment dynamics for the same contrast and aggregation as the post-treatment result. A nonrejected pre-trend test establishes its observed test outcome; assess a claimed economically small pre-trend against a stated magnitude and its interval. Select a concurrent-policy or placebo comparison when it distinguishes a specified alternative causal path. Preserve the outcome scale: parallel trends in levels and in logarithms impose different restrictions.

## Orthogonal scores and heterogeneous effects

Select the target and orthogonal score before the nuisance learners. In `DoubleML`, a partially linear score targets its partially linear coefficient, while an interactive-regression score targets the configured ATE or ATT; an instrumental score adds instrument conditions. Cross-fitting estimates nuisance functions away from each scoring fold. Split at the independent sampling or assignment unit, retain required panel/cluster structure and keep tuning inside the training folds. Machine learning supplies nuisance estimates; the design supplies identification.

For `grf::causal_forest`, binary unconfounded treatment targets a conditional average effect, and continuous treatment targets the conditional covariance-to-variance slope under its stated treatment model. Choose the forest and aggregation for the requested population. Cluster weighting and unit weighting define different population averages. When the result allocates treatment, calculate value net of treatment and capacity costs on the evaluation population; train and evaluate the policy on separate information. Select heterogeneity contrasts for the competing mechanism or action threshold they resolve.

## Structural estimation and counterfactuals

Write the economic model, observation process and identifying restrictions together. Identify which prices, characteristics, costs and decisions are jointly determined. Map each parameter to observed variation, excluded shifters or an explicit calibration. A fitted demand curve and an equilibrium inversion recover different objects. Choose additional structure for the parameter or counterfactual it identifies, and compare competing conduct or behavioral restrictions where they produce different observations.

For differentiated-product demand, define markets, choice sets, outside shares and price endogeneity; use excluded variation and substitution patterns to identify price sensitivity and heterogeneity. For multiproduct pricing, write q(p)−Δ(p−mc)=0 with Δ_jk=−H_jk∂q_k/∂p_j and the adopted ownership/conduct matrix H. Recover markups and marginal costs using the estimated demand derivatives, then re-solve prices and quantities after the intervention. Use `PyBLP` when its demand/supply system implements that model. Carry product withdrawal, entry or ownership changes into available choices and the equilibrium system.

A calculation holding baseline shares and demand derivatives fixed supplies a local pricing approximation. For a requested equilibrium after a finite ownership or cost change, solve the new pricing equations and recompute shares, profits and surplus in that same scenario through the [specialist result methods](../../econ-data/references/implementation.md#estimator-engine-and-algorithm-selection). When comparing welfare across dates or markets, establish the outside option's change: a rising inside share can reflect improved inside products or a deteriorating alternative. Holding outside utility constant is a substantive restriction for that welfare comparison.

For dynamic choice or games, define the state, action, transitions, payoff normalization, discount factor and equilibrium selection. Estimate state transitions from the corresponding transitions in the data. Select a full-solution likelihood, conditional-choice-probability or simulation-based method according to the model and identifying restrictions. Re-solving the model under changed policy requires the specified invariant primitives and equilibrium rule. Use the official replication or established numerical library for the selected model and solve the original Bellman, moment or equilibrium conditions to the calculation's adopted tolerance.

For investment or employment adjustment, distinguish the cost of changing each unit from a fixed cost of adjusting at all and from irreversibility. Compare adjustment with waiting or inaction before fitting a smooth adjustment equation. Connecting market-to-book value to the marginal value of capital requires the production, competition and adjustment-cost restrictions that equate them. A cash-flow coefficient can reflect information about investment opportunities left out of that proxy. To identify a financing mechanism, select variation in available finance that separates it from expected profitability and derive its effect on the chosen investment margin.

## Random assignment and interventions

Specify the parameter's assignment unit, mechanism, stratification and probabilities. Explain how implementation, take-up, attrition and spillovers enter the comparison. Distinguish assignment effects from treatment-receipt effects. Define receipt estimands through their compliance assumptions. Match adjustment, weighting, randomization inference and cluster inference to the assignment structure.

## Regression discontinuity

Specify the running variable, cutoff, assignment rule, compliance, support and local parameter. Connect continuity or local randomization to manipulation, heaping, discretization and other cutoff rules. Specify the result's bandwidth, polynomial, kernel, bias correction and uncertainty. For a fuzzy design, connect outcome and treatment jumps to the local compliance parameter.

## Instrumental variables and assignment leniency

Define the treatment, instrument, first stage and target population. Explain the economic paths that support independence, exclusion and monotonicity. Assess instrument strength with diagnostics matched to the model and inference procedure. When conventional inference relies on a strong first stage that the design does not establish, use an inference procedure valid under weak identification.

For a new participation policy, derive who changes treatment under that policy. Under the instrument conditions above and policy-invariant potential outcomes, write the latent-index model D=1{U≤p} conditional on the adopted covariates, with U uniform on [0,1] and MTE(u)=E[Y₁−Y₀|U=u]. A shift p₀→p₁, with p₁>p₀, changes the population mean by ∫[p₀,p₁] MTE(u)du; dividing by p₁−p₀ gives the effect per induced participant. An instrument moving a different interval identifies a different weighted effect. Match the new interval to identified support before applying the estimate. If two effect schedules fit the historical comparison and reverse the new action's ranking, obtain variation at the new margin or carry their compatible implications into the stated decision criterion. A more precise historical LATE leaves that difference unresolved.

For judge, examiner or officer leniency, establish actual assignment and within-cell variation. Choose the leave-out unit from that assignment structure. Establish how instrument-estimation error, examiner sample size, treatment dimensions, sorting and monotonicity enter the requested parameter. Map that parameter to its compliance population and institutional margin.

## Shift-share designs

Identify whether shocks or shares support exogeneity. Specify share dates, shocks, concentration, exposure, leave-out construction, common components and effective assignment units. When a coefficient combines many comparisons, inspect its influential weights and examine the competing outcome paths of the components that drive it. Match inference to shock dependence and exposure overlap. Define the estimand through the chosen exogeneity argument.

## Networks and spillovers

Define the relationship, measurement date, exposure mapping, own and peers' treatments, and interference set for the requested effect. Distinguish common shocks, network formation, endogenous peer selection, direct effects and spillovers through the design. Trace assignment to downstream exposure. Establish how later treatment, migration, link changes and outcome recording enter the effect. Match inference to assignment and dependence.

## Synthetic controls and panel counterfactuals

Define the treated unit, donors, pre-treatment support and counterfactual structure. Establish how fit, contamination, extrapolation and regularization enter the estimate. Select placebo or sensitivity exercises to examine the specified identifying premise. Explain uncertainty through the assignment model, sampling framework or inferential procedure used.

## Inference and interpretation

Choose standard errors, clustering, randomization inference or another justified procedure from the design's sampling and assignment variation. Map effective assignment units, dependence and small-sample corrections to that procedure. Preserve prespecified outcome families and multiple-testing adjustments. Use direct contrasts for subgroup differences. For a sensitivity analysis, choose departures from the identifying assumption that represent the suspected economic confounder, find the departure that changes the conclusion, and express it in the outcome's units over the period being evaluated.

Report the defined parameter and its numerical uncertainty directly. Assess the named design through its comparison, estimand and assignment mechanism. For a requested repair, define the resulting comparison and estimand and identify the assignment mechanism and reported comparison. ## Connect observed choices and outcomes to the model and interventions

## Construct restrictions and set-valued predictions

Use the branch matching the supplied choices, budgets or equilibrium observations and the requested target.

- **Turn feasible deviations into valid observable inequalities.** Write chosen minus deviating payoff Δπ; under expected-payoff best response, derive E[Δπ | J] ≥ 0 conditional on actor information J. Hold simultaneous opponents' actions fixed; include induced continuation choices in sequential deviations. For measurable payoff difference Δr and each nonnegative information-measurable weight h, derive the error restriction yielding E[hΔr(θ)] ≥ 0. Private choice-relevant shocks can remain selected: entry optimality (2d−1)(xβ+δd_other+ε) ≥ 0 does not allow deleting ε merely because E[ε]=0. For profit R(d)−d(θ+η), common feasible add/remove deviations and the relevant zero-mean error restrictions yield E[R(d+1)−R(d)] ≤ θ ≤ E[R(d)−R(d−1)]. Nonnegative instruments with E[η|x]=0 give weighted bounds, which can be intersected. At d=0 removal is infeasible; retain valid one-sided restrictions or derive a justified boundary correction. Trimming zero choices can invalidate the mean restriction. Adjacent deviations establish global optimality only with additional shape such as discrete concavity.

- **Eliminate selection through equilibrium-region probabilities.** Map latent payoff states into their permitted equilibrium outcomes. In a complete-information two-firm pure-entry model with strategic substitutes and zero-probability payoff ties, let q₁₀ be the probability that (1,0) is the unique equilibrium and q_S the probability that the equilibrium set is {(1,0),(0,1)}. Then observed p₁₀ = q₁₀ + λq_S with 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1 gives q₁₀ ≤ p₁₀ ≤ q₁₀+q_S. Combine these bounds with the other outcomes' probabilities and the maintained latent-state distribution. In larger games, impose one jointly feasible selection across outcomes; individual-outcome bounds alone can describe only an outer region. Realized mixed-strategy actions need the corresponding probabilistic mapping. A sharper region requires a substantive restriction on payoffs, shock distributions, support or selection; establish compatibility for every retained specification before claiming sharpness.

- **Carry the joint compatible set into the requested counterfactual.** For every jointly admissible primitive vector and permitted baseline/policy equilibrium selection, re-solve under the justified invariant primitives and map the result into T. Bound T over that joint set, preserving justified dependence across scenarios. Separate coefficient intervals generally contain combinations that the joint restrictions exclude. Historical selection need not survive a policy change, so even identified payoffs can imply multiple counterfactual outcomes. If all feasible T are positive, the sign is resolved. When the task asks for an action and rankings vary, specify its decision criterion: maximin maximizes worst-case welfare, whereas minimax regret minimizes the largest loss relative to the best action in each compatible state. Sampling uncertainty requires its own inferential treatment.

- **Use observed budgets to construct rationalizing preferences.** For error-free price/bundle observations (pᵗ,xᵗ) sharing stable preferences, construct the Afriat feasibility system u_s ≤ u_t + λ_t pᵗ·(xˢ−xᵗ), λ_t > 0, for every s,t. With standard positive prices and consumption budgets, feasibility characterizes locally nonsatiated utility rationalization and supplies a monotone concave continuous representation. Pooling different people imposes common preferences. For a new budget {x≥0:p·x≤m}, restrict candidates to p·x=m, then append each candidate choice and its inequalities; retain choices whose expanded system is feasible under the maintained environment. This derives a counterfactual set without selecting a parametric utility. Noisy observations require an explicit observation model, and feasible rationalization leaves multiple utilities available.
