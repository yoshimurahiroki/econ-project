Housing Supply and the Geography of Commuting
We develop and quantify a spatial model in which workers choose where to live and where to work and landlords supply floor space with location-specific elasticities. We estimate the model on commuting flows between 1,214 neighborhoods of Port Halvard using mobile-phone records for 2.8 million workers. Housing supply elasticities range from 0.4 in the urban core to 2.1 at the periphery, and commuting costs rise by 1.9 percent per kilometer. A new metro line raises aggregate welfare by 1.3 percent, and landowners in the 12 neighborhoods within 800 meters of a station capture 46 percent of the gain. Doubling the supply elasticity in the core lifts the welfare gain to 2.2 percent. The model rationalizes the gradient of rents with respect to commuting access and delivers closed-form counterfactuals through changes in equilibrium variables.
KEYWORDS: Spatial economics, commuting, housing supply, transit, quantitative model.
