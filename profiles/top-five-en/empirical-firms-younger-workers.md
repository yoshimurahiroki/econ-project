VI. Effects on Firms and Younger Workers
A. First Stage and Hiring
Exposure moves retention in every year after 2017. An increase of 1 percentage point in exposure raises the number of retained workers per 100 employees by 0.38 (s.e. 0.02), and the first-stage F-statistic equals 412. At the mean exposure of 6.7 percent, the reform retains 2.55 older workers per 100 employees.
Table 3 reports the estimates of equation (6). Column 1 reports the ordinary least squares estimate of the effect of retained workers on hires under 30, which equals −0.05 (s.e. 0.02). Column 2 reports the instrumental variables estimate, which equals −0.31 (s.e. 0.07). The ordinary least squares estimate sits closer to zero because firms with rising demand retain more older workers and hire more young workers. At the mean exposure of 2.55 retained workers per 100 employees, the reform lowers hires under 30 by 0.79 per 100 employees, which equals 10.1 percent of the mean of 7.8.

B. Event Study
Figure 4 plots the instrumental variables estimates of the effect on hires under 30 for each year, obtained by scaling the reduced-form coefficient θk of equation (7) by the first-stage coefficient of year k. The estimates for 2013 through 2016 lie between −0.05 and 0.04, with a joint p-value of 0.64. The estimates for 2018 through 2022 equal −0.27 (s.e. 0.09), −0.30 (s.e. 0.08), −0.33 (s.e. 0.08), −0.31 (s.e. 0.08), and −0.32 (s.e. 0.09). The effect starts in the first year after the first treated cohort reaches age 60 and stays within 0.04 of the pooled estimate in each later year.
C. Robustness
Table 4 reports six variations of the specification and one placebo. Each variation returns an estimate between −0.34 and −0.29. Sector-by-region-by-year effects give −0.29 (s.e. 0.07). Restricting the sample to firms with 20 or more employees gives −0.33 (s.e. 0.08), and restricting it to private-sector firms gives −0.32 (s.e. 0.07). A 12-month window for exposure gives −0.34 (s.e. 0.08), and two-way clustering by firm and sector gives −0.31 (s.e. 0.09). The placebo assigns exposure from the cohorts born in 1953 and 1954, who reach age 60 in 2013 and 2014 under the old eligibility age, and returns 0.01 (s.e. 0.05).

D. Younger Workers and the Aggregate
The decline in youth hiring lowers the earnings of entrants. We assign each worker aged 25 to 29 the exposure of the sector-region cell that the worker enters and estimate a regression analogous to equation (6) on earnings. Entrants in 2018 through 2020 earn 1,570 kora (s.e. 380) less per year in their first two years of employment. Their cumulative loss over five years equals 5,900 kora (s.e. 1,350), or 41 percent of the median annual earnings of 14,400 kora at ages 25 to 29.
The aggregate effects follow from the estimates. The reform retains 64,200 workers aged 60 to 62 in 2022. The displacement ratio of 0.31 lowers hires of workers under 30 by 19,900, and employment rises by 44,300, or 6.9 persons per 10 retained workers.
