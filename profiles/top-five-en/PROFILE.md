# English economics writing profiles

Profile family: top-five-en. Language: English.

## Use

Every English sentence, section architecture, table, figure and slide is borrowed from the corresponding passage of a real Top Five paper or seminar deck in the user's connected document library and adjusted only for the study's own facts, names, numbers, units and citations.

1. Select the original closest to the study in field and design, then the passage that performs the same function as the passage being written: abstract, introduction paragraph, institutional setting, data, framework, empirical strategy, results, mechanism, welfare, conclusion, table notes, figure notes or slide.
2. Read the passage in the original PDF with its surrounding pages.
3. Copy its sentences into the private working record by function and draft each sentence by substituting the study's facts into one copied sentence.
4. Leave out content with no corresponding source sentence.

The source sheet, the identity of each original and every mapping stay in the private working record. The conventions below fix notation, vocabulary, sentence, paragraph, section, whole-document, exhibit, layout and slide settings for English output; the sentences themselves come from English originals by the procedure above.

## 1. Characters and notation

Use US English, ordinary Latin letters, one space between words, and one space after sentence punctuation. Use periods for sentences, commas for clauses and lists, a serial comma, and parentheses for numerical inference and definitions. Use curly quotation marks and apostrophes. Use an en dash for ranges and the mathematical minus sign − for negative values. Established compound terms use a hyphen. Acronyms use capital letters; define the full term and acronym at first occurrence. Use US, EU, and et al.

Use digits for measurements, counts, dates, section labels, and ordered results. Begin a numerical sentence with its subject. Write a leading zero before a decimal, group integers with commas from 4 digits, and write years as 4 ungrouped digits. Dates follow YYYY-MM-DD. Insert a space between a number and its unit. Use percent in prose, % in compact displays, and percentage points for a change in a proportion.

| Quantity | Decimal places |
|---|---:|
| Counts | 0 |
| Monetary amounts in the stated currency unit | 0 |
| Percentages and percentage-point changes | 1 |
| Coefficients, standard errors, and interval endpoints | 2 |
| Ratios, elasticities, correlations, and multipliers | 2 |
| Probabilities and p-values | 3 |

Retain full precision in the result record. Use round-half-to-even for displays. For a positive p-value below 0.001, display p < 0.001. A coefficient, its standard error, and its interval use a common scale and precision. Display a currency code with its amount; record the price base and scale beside the first monetary result.

## 2. Words and subjects

Use one preferred term for each concept throughout a document. Inflect that term for grammatical number. The measurement vocabulary includes estimate, estimator, parameter, coefficient, sample, mean, variance, outcome, standard errors, and confidence interval.

| Field | Preferred vocabulary |
|---|---|
| Labor | wage, employment, earnings, worker, firm, productivity |
| Industrial organization | demand, supply, price, cost, competition, consumer, market, profit |
| Spatial economics | location, region, housing, migration, city, population |
| Macroeconomics | consumption, investment, capital, income, growth, equilibrium |
| Econometrics | treatment effect, identification, inference, instrumental variable, fixed effects, counterfactual |
| Welfare and policy | welfare, surplus, utility, benefit, policy |

Use estimate for estimation, identify for identification, document for a descriptive finding, calculate for arithmetic and compute for algorithmic evaluation, construct for a research object, and compare for a comparison. Use we for research operations and findings; use this paper for the research objective. Give the economic actor as the subject of a behavioral statement. Describe the measured relationship or quantity when motivating the question.

## 3. Phrases and quantitative statements

Order a quantitative statement as object → change or estimate → value → unit → numerical inference. Establish the population and period in the immediately preceding sentence. Put the benchmark conversion in the next sentence: baseline → ratio or multiplier → interpretation. Compute a difference by subtraction, division for a ratio, and division by the baseline followed by multiplication by 100 for a percentage change.

## 4. Sentences

Use one proposition per sentence and up to 5 numerical values. Pair an estimate with its inference in the same sentence. Place an adjectival modifier immediately before its noun, with up to 8 words. Place prepositional and relative modifiers after the noun. Place the actor before its action and put the principal verb within the first 10 words.

| Sentence function | Words |
|---|---:|
| Question, motivation, contribution, conclusion | 12–28 |
| Institution, source, sample, variable definition | 10–26 |
| Method, estimating equation, identifying variation, inference | 12–30 |
| Result, heterogeneity, mechanism, counterfactual, welfare | 12–30 |
| Roadmap | 3–20 |
| Transition | 8–20 |

Use the present for research operations, definitions, model relations, and exhibit descriptions. Use the past for realized outcomes, completed interventions, and completed data collection. A finding frame takes a proposition with the tense of its underlying object. Select the fixed words and order from the catalog; fill its typed slots with the registered scientific content.

## 5. Sentence connections

Move from an established object to its new property, then to its consequence. Use 0–2 explicit connectors per paragraph. Use First, Second, and Finally for a sequence; For example for an illustration; We then or We next for an operation; and In addition for another result on the current object.

Attach this or these to a named object in the preceding sentence. Keep a pronoun’s antecedent within 1 sentence. Define a symbol before its first use, with a gap of up to 2 sentences. Restate the definition when a new section returns to that symbol. Carry every conversion through the same saved result ID and unit map.

## 6. Paragraphs

Give each paragraph one main function. Use 3–6 sentences and 70–150 words; a definition paragraph uses 2–4 sentences and 40–100 words. Start with its object or proposition and end with the result that advances the next paragraph. Use up to 12 numerical values, 2 exhibit references, 2 citation groups, and 3 cited works per paragraph.

Use these functional sequences: question → economic relationship → measured scale; source → population → period → measurement; estimating object → operation → implementation; result → numerical magnitude → comparison → mechanism; counterfactual → allocation → welfare measure → quantity. Fill every component with its applicable sentence frames. Apply the one-proposition rule to each sentence in a paragraph or section frame.

## 7. Sections

Use noun-phrase headings of 1–6 words and up to 45 characters. Number sections 1, 2, 3 and subsections 2.1, 2.2. Give each subsection one analytical object. Split a subsection when its object changes or its text exceeds 900 words. Place its defining equation after the definitions and place an exhibit immediately after its first substantive reference.

The introduction follows question → economic scale → institutional variation → data → method → 3 principal results → contribution → roadmap. Give each principal result its own paragraph. Open subsequent sections with their object and close with the result used by the next section.

## 8. Whole papers

The following shares apply to main-text words. Front matter, references, and appendices have separate budgets.

| Genre | Words | Ordered sections and shares | Tables / figures |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Reduced form | 6,000–9,000 | Introduction 12%; institution 8%; data 12%; method 18%; results 30%; mechanisms 12%; conclusion 8% | 4 / 4 |
| Structural | 7,000–11,000 | Introduction 10%; environment 8%; data 12%; model 20%; estimation 15%; fit 10%; counterfactuals 20%; conclusion 5% | 5 / 5 |
| Theory | 5,000–8,000 | Introduction 12%; model 18%; results 35%; extensions 20%; illustration 10%; conclusion 5% | 1 / 3 |
| Econometric method | 6,000–9,000 | Introduction 12%; setup 15%; construction 18%; formal results 25%; simulations 15%; application 10%; conclusion 5% | 4 / 4 |
| Policy evaluation | 5,000–7,500 | Introduction 10%; institution 15%; data 15%; method 15%; results 30%; costs and welfare 10%; conclusion 5% | 3 / 4 |
| Survey | 6,000–10,000 | Question and scope 10%; conceptual map 20%; evidence 40%; synthesis 20%; research agenda 10% | 4 / 3 |
| Short paper | 2,000–3,500 | Introduction 20%; data 20%; method 20%; results 30%; conclusion 10% | 2 / 2 |

Use a noun-phrase title of 8–14 words and up to 100 characters. Put authors on one line and affiliations on the next. Write a 6-sentence abstract of 120–180 words: question → data or model → method → main result → mechanism or welfare → contribution. Add 4–6 keywords, 2–4 classification codes, and an acknowledgment paragraph of 40–80 words.

The conclusion contains 3 paragraphs: answer and magnitude → economic mechanism → implication. In a survey, place these paragraphs at the end of the research agenda. Place references after the concluding section, followed by appendices and supplementary files. Order exhibits as data → main estimates → heterogeneity → mechanism or counterfactual. Number them at first reference. Use appendices A, B, C; appendix equations (A.1), tables A1, and figures A1. Register every symbol, term, quantity, unit, population, and period once and reuse its value throughout.

## 9. Tables, figures, equations, and citations

Tables have a numbered noun-phrase title, outcome labels above columns, and column numbers (1), (2), (3). Put the coefficient and its parenthesized standard error on consecutive rows. Follow coefficient rows with observations, fixed effects, controls, sample, and weights. Use X for an included fixed-effect or control block. Align numbers at the decimal point and place horizontal rules at the table boundaries and below the header.

Order table notes as content → column estimators → fixed effects → sample → standard-error unit → weights. Use 2–4 sentences and up to 80 words. Supply each item from the saved specification or result.

Figures use a numbered noun-phrase title, quantity-and-unit axis labels, and a horizontal legend above the plotting region. Use blue #0072B2 for the main series and orange #D55E00 for a comparison; pair colors with distinct marker shapes. Draw lines at 1.6pt. Draw a 95% interval as whiskers or a band with opacity 0.15 and label its level. Order figure notes as content → sample → regression specification → interval construction, using up to 60 words.

Use i for an observational unit, t for time, j for a group or alternative, β for a coefficient, and ε for a disturbance. Define each symbol in the sentence that introduces it, and define the symbols of a displayed equation in the "where" clause that follows it. Number displayed equations consecutively at the right margin and cite them as equation (1). Bind each theoretical statement to its named model and defined parameter domain.

Use Author (Year) when the cited work is the grammatical subject and (Author Year) for supporting attribution. Write both names for 2 authors and the first surname plus et al. for 3 or more. Order grouped citations by year, then surname, separated by semicolons. Alphabetize the bibliography. Article entries contain author → year → quoted title → italic journal → volume (issue) → pages → DOI. Book entries contain author → year → italic title → edition → publisher. Working-paper entries contain author → year → quoted title → series and identifier → institution → version date → URL.

Set tables in portrait orientation with at most 9 columns including row headings. Use horizontal rules and blank space only, with no shading. Spell out column headings in full. Divide a table into Panel A, Panel B. Place a zero before the decimal point (0.357). Report standard errors in parentheses and use no significance asterisks. Key notes on specific entries with lowercase letters (a, b, c) and place the source note after the other notes. Supply figures as vector PDF and label panels a, b, c. Designate appendices A, B, C and number their equations, theorems and propositions (A1), (A2).

## 10. Paper layout

Set papers in the Econometrica template of tex/paper (econsocart, LuaLaTeX). Place a table title above the table and a figure title below the figure, each exhibit after the paragraph that first cites it. Center page numbers at the foot.

## 11. Research slides

Use 13 main slides for 20 minutes, plus 6 backup slides. Allocate time in minutes as follows: title 0.5; motivation 1.5; question and study 1; result preview 1; institution 1.5; data 1.5; model 2; identifying variation and estimation 2; main result 2.5; heterogeneity 1.5; mechanism 1.5; counterfactual and welfare 2; conclusion 1.5.

Use a 16:9 canvas of 960 × 540pt, body text at 28pt, headings at 32pt, and the title at 40pt; Japanese decks use 32pt, 36pt and 44pt. Set side margins to 54pt, the top margin to 36pt, and the bottom margin to 30pt. Use the metropolis theme and Waseda colors of tex/slides. Put the page number at the lower right.

Give each slide one main proposition and up to 2 supporting statements. Use a noun-phrase heading of up to 8 words and 52 characters. Keep body text within 6 lines, 11 words per line, and 55 words per slide. Use up to 3 top-level bullets and 2 levels of hierarchy. Use noun phrases for labels and finite clauses for findings, with one grammatical form within a list. Bullets end at their final word. Use “item: content” for parallel measurement or specification entries.

A figure slide gives 70% of its content area to the exhibit and places 1–2 interpretation lines below it. Put the unit on the axis and the population beside the exhibit. Use 28pt labels and legend text. An equation slide gives the equation first and symbol definitions next, with up to 3 definitions. Backup slides contain additional estimates, alternative displays, and calculation details.

## 12. Additional genres

| Genre | Size | Ordered content |
|---|---|---|
| Research brief or policy report | 600–900 words, 2 pages | Conclusion; numerical evidence; mechanism; quantity-and-action table |
| Research proposal | 1,000–1,500 words | Question; data; identifying variation; planned quantities; milestones |
| Referee response | 100–180 words per item | Actionable comment; response; exact section and page of the change |
| Meeting material | 300–600 words, 2 pages | Previous decisions; progress; new results; decisions required; next tasks |
| Paper explanation | 5 paragraphs, 350–500 words | Question; economic relevance; method; result; contribution |

The proposal records target sample size, intervention scale, detectable effect, and interval width from the design. Use an 8-week schedule with milestones at weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8 for data preparation, estimation, interpretation, and delivery. A response quotes up to 25 words of its actionable comment. A meeting action records its owner, deliverable, and date.

## 13. English grammar and consistency

Target 75–90% active finite clauses. Use the passive when the measured object supplies the paragraph’s continuing topic. Use a or an for a first singular count noun, the for an established object, and zero article for a plural or mass noun used generally. Keep a singular estimand with a singular verb and plural observations with a plural verb. Treat data as plural, evidence as a mass noun, and percentage points as a plural unit.

Keep research operations in the present, completed events in the past, and model relationships in the present. Define an acronym at its first occurrence in the abstract and again in the main text. Preserve one spelling, term, symbol, unit, and numerical display for each registered object. English frames and English terminology supply the English document from its own source record.
