Public Ratings with Heterogeneous Buyers
I. Model
A sender and a continuum of buyers interact once. The state ω ∈ {0, 1} equals 1 with probability μ ∈ (0, 1), the prior. A rating is a pair (S, π), where S is a finite set of grades and π(· | ω) is a probability distribution over S for each state ω. The sender commits to a rating before the state is realized. A buyer with threshold t ∈ [0, 1] observes the grade s, forms the posterior ps = Pr(ω = 1 | s) by Bayes' rule, and buys if ps ≥ t. Thresholds are distributed across buyers according to a continuous cumulative distribution function G on [0, 1] with G(0) = 0 and G(1) = 1, and G has density g wherever it is differentiable. The sender's payoff equals the mass of buyers who buy.
A rating induces a distribution of posteriors τ on [0, 1]. The sender's payoff from a rating that induces τ equals
V(τ) = ∫ G(p) dτ(p).   (1)
A distribution τ is Bayes consistent if its mean equals μ. Let cav G denote the concave envelope of G, the smallest concave function on [0, 1] that lies on or above G.
LEMMA 1: A distribution τ with finite support is induced by a rating if and only if τ is Bayes consistent.
PROOF: The law of iterated expectations gives E[ps] = μ for every rating, which establishes necessity. For sufficiency, let τ place probability λi on the posterior pi for i = 1, …, n, with Σi λi pi = μ. Define π(si | 1) = λi pi / μ and π(si | 0) = λi(1 − pi) / (1 − μ). Both are probability distributions, because Σi λi pi / μ = 1 and Σi λi(1 − pi) / (1 − μ) = 1. Grade si occurs with probability μ π(si | 1) + (1 − μ) π(si | 0) = λi, and its posterior equals μ π(si | 1) / λi = pi. Q.E.D.
II. Optimal Ratings
THEOREM 1: The sender's optimal value equals cav G(μ). There exist posteriors a ≤ μ ≤ b with cav G(a) = G(a) and cav G(b) = G(b) such that cav G is affine on [a, b]. If a = b = μ, the uninformative rating attains the value. If a < b, the rating that induces a with probability (b − μ)/(b − a) and b with probability (μ − a)/(b − a) attains the value. If a > 0, b < 1, and G is differentiable at a and b, then
g(a) = g(b) = (G(b) − G(a)) / (b − a).   (2)
PROOF: Consider any Bayes consistent τ. Because cav G ≥ G and cav G is concave, Jensen's inequality gives V(τ) = ∫ G dτ ≤ ∫ cav G dτ ≤ cav G(∫ p dτ) = cav G(μ). Lemma 1 extends the bound to every rating. The upper boundary of the convex hull of the graph of G consists of points of the graph and chords between two points of the graph, and cav G traces this boundary. Hence cav G(μ) = λ G(a) + (1 − λ) G(b) for a pair a ≤ μ ≤ b and a weight λ ∈ [0, 1] with λ a + (1 − λ) b = μ, and cav G is affine on [a, b] with cav G(a) = G(a) and cav G(b) = G(b). The distribution with mass λ on a and 1 − λ on b is Bayes consistent, and Lemma 1 delivers a rating that induces it. Its value equals λ G(a) + (1 − λ) G(b) = cav G(μ), which equals the upper bound. For equation (2), the function cav G − G is nonnegative and equals zero at a and b. At an interior point where G is differentiable, this function attains a minimum, so its derivative equals zero and g equals the slope of cav G. The slope of cav G on [a, b] equals (G(b) − G(a))/(b − a). Q.E.D.
The theorem reduces the design of the rating to the concavification of the threshold distribution. The result reflects a trade between the mass of buyers served at a posterior and the probability of reaching it. Raising the upper posterior from a to b brings the buyers with thresholds in [a, b] into the market and lowers the probability of the upper grade, because posteriors average to the prior. The optimal pair equates the density of the marginal buyer at each posterior with the slope of the chord, which gives equation (2).
COROLLARY 1: A rating with two grades attains the value of every rating.
PROOF: Theorem 1 constructs a rating with two grades whose value equals cav G(μ), the optimal value over all ratings. Q.E.D.
An example fixes ideas. Thresholds are uniform on [0.4, 0.8] and the prior equals μ = 0.5. The concave envelope equals cav G(t) = 1.25 t on [0, 0.8] and 1 on [0.8, 1]. The optimal posteriors are a = 0 and b = 0.8, and G has a kink at b. The upper grade occurs with probability 0.5/0.8 = 0.625, and every buyer buys after the upper grade, so the optimal value equals 0.625. An uninformative rating yields G(0.5) = 0.25, and full disclosure yields 0.5.
III. Benchmarks and Comparative Statics
PROPOSITION 2: (a) Full disclosure is optimal at every prior if and only if G(t) ≤ t for every t ∈ [0, 1]. (b) The uninformative rating is optimal at every prior if and only if G is concave. (c) The optimal value cav G(μ) is concave and nondecreasing in μ.
PROOF: (a) Full disclosure induces the posteriors 0 and 1 with probabilities 1 − μ and μ, and its value equals μ G(1) + (1 − μ) G(0) = μ. Suppose G(t) ≤ t for every t. The identity function is concave and lies on or above G, so cav G(μ) ≤ μ. Full disclosure attains μ, so cav G(μ) = μ and full disclosure is optimal. For necessity, suppose G(t0) > t0 at a point t0. At the prior μ = t0, cav G(t0) ≥ G(t0) > t0, which exceeds the value of full disclosure. (b) The uninformative rating induces the posterior μ and yields G(μ). It is optimal at every prior if and only if G(μ) = cav G(μ) for every μ, which holds if and only if G is concave. (c) The function cav G is concave by construction. The constant function 1 is concave and lies on or above G, so cav G ≤ 1, and cav G(1) ≥ G(1) = 1. A concave function on [0, 1] that attains its maximum at 1 is nondecreasing. Q.E.D.
The first benchmark covers threshold distributions that first-order stochastically dominate the uniform distribution on [0, 1]. Buyers in these populations hold high thresholds, and the sender reveals the state because certification at a posterior of 1 reaches every buyer. The second benchmark covers threshold distributions with a nonincreasing density. Buyers in these populations concentrate at low thresholds, and the sender sells to them at the prior.
COROLLARY 2: The marginal value of prior quality equals the slope of cav G at μ, which equals (G(b) − G(a))/(b − a) whenever a < b, and the marginal value declines in μ.
The corollary follows from Theorem 1 and Proposition 2(c). In the uniform example, the marginal value of prior quality equals 1.25 for every prior below 0.8, so each 0.01 increase in the prior raises sales by 1.25 buyers per 100 buyers.
