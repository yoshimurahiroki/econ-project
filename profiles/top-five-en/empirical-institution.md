I. The Pension Age Act and the Marden Labor Market
A. The Marden Pension System
Marden operates a three-tier pension system. The first tier, the public old-age pension, pays a benefit equal to 62 percent of average lifetime earnings with a ceiling of 31,000 kora and is financed by an 18 percent payroll tax. The second tier consists of occupational plans that cover 58 percent of employees. The third tier holds private savings. The public pension reaches 94 percent of retirees and supplies 71 percent of the income of the median household with a head aged 65 or older.
Eligibility for the public pension depends on the age of the claimant. Before 2012, every resident qualified at age 60. The Marden Social Insurance Agency (MSIA) accepts claims from the first day of the month after the claimant's birthday at the eligibility age and rejects claims filed earlier. A claimant who defers receives an increment of 5.2 percent of the benefit for each year of deferral. Workers combine pension receipt with earnings, and 31 percent of pension recipients aged 60 to 62 hold a job.
B. The Pension Age Act of 2011
The Assembly of Marden passed the Pension Age Act on March 14, 2011 by a vote of 61 to 39, and the Act took effect on January 1, 2012. The Act raises the eligibility age from 60 to 63 in one step for every worker born on or after January 1, 1958. Workers born earlier keep the eligibility age of 60. The Act contains zero phase-in provisions, so a worker born on December 31, 1957 and a worker born on January 1, 1958 face eligibility ages of 60 and 63. The government justified the Act with a projected deficit of 2.3 percent of GDP in the public pension fund by 2030.
The Act arrived as a surprise to workers in the affected cohorts. The 2009 election platforms of all five parties omitted the reform, and the draft reached the Assembly 11 weeks before the vote. Workers born in 1958 turned 53 in 2011, seven years before they reached the old eligibility age of 60, and the earliest affected claim date fell in 2018.
C. Employment Contracts and Promotion Ladders
Collective agreements set the wage scales of 71 percent of Marden employees. The Marden Employers Federation and the Federation of Trade Unions negotiate sector-wide scales every three years, and the scales assign each job a wage by rung, age band, and tenure. The entry wage for a full-time position in the sample equals 9,100 kora, and the wage at the top rung of the median ladder equals 19,400 kora. Firms hold zero discretion over the entry wage in covered sectors and set it freely in the remaining sectors.
Firms organize positions into promotion ladders with 4 to 9 rungs. A firm fills a vacancy at rung k by promoting an employee from rung k − 1 in 82 percent of cases and by external hire in 18 percent of cases, so a vacancy at the top of the ladder triggers a chain of promotions that ends with a hire at the entry rung. The chain connects the exit of an older worker at the top to the hiring of a young worker at the bottom. A worker who postpones exit by one year postpones the chain by one year.
D. Disability and Unemployment Insurance
Two programs pay benefits to workers aged 60 to 62 who leave employment before the pension age. Disability insurance (DI) awards benefits after a medical assessment and replaces 54 percent of earnings. Unemployment insurance (UI) pays 48 percent of earnings for 26 months to workers who lose a job involuntarily and register for work. Together the programs paid benefits to 14.5 percent of workers aged 60 to 62 in 2017. The programs supply the replacement income that a worker uses when the pension age rises.
