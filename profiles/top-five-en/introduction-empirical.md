Delayed Retirement and the Employment of Younger Workers
Population aging pushes governments to extend working lives. Between 2005 and 2022, 14 of the 20 member states of the Meridian Economic Council raised the age at which workers qualify for a public pension, and official projections of pension spending in each state assume that older workers who stay employed add to total employment. The assumption invites a prediction from the demand side of the labor market. If firms hold the size of their workforce fixed, every older worker who stays takes the position of a younger worker, and the reform moves employment across age groups at zero net gain. Trade unions, employer federations, and legislators cite this prediction in every national debate on pension ages. The size of the displacement of younger workers by retained older workers is the central empirical quantity for the design of these reforms.
This paper measures the displacement. We ask how many hires of younger workers a firm gives up for each older worker it retains after a rise in the pension eligibility age. The answer pins down the net employment effect of the reform, the incidence of the reform across age groups, and the margin on which firms adjust when the age composition of their workforce shifts. Estimation requires exogenous variation in the retention of older workers and records that link every worker to an employer over time. We use both.
The variation comes from the Pension Age Act of 2011 in Marden, a country of 9.6 million residents. The Act raises the age of eligibility for the public old-age pension from 60 to 63 for every worker born on or after January 1, 1958, and holds the age at 60 for every worker born earlier. A worker born on December 31, 1957 qualifies for the pension in 2017, and a worker born one day later qualifies in 2021. Birth date determines eligibility. Cohorts born on either side of the cutoff share the same labor market, the same firms, and the same macroeconomic conditions, and the cutoff assigns eligibility ages of 60 and 63 to workers born on consecutive days.
We combine the universe of administrative records of the Marden Social Insurance Agency with the employer-employee panel of the Marden Revenue Service for 2010 through 2022. The records report monthly employment spells, annual earnings, pension claims, and the employer of every worker, together with annual payroll and hiring for each of 412,000 firms. The estimation sample contains 452,310 workers born within 18 months of the cutoff and 187,400 firms with 10 or more employees. The data trace each firm's hiring in each age group from the year before the first treated cohort reaches age 60 through the year after the last treated cohort reaches age 62.
The cutoff delivers causal effects because birth dates are fixed before the Act passes. The youngest worker in the sample was born 51 years and 9 months before the Act became law. The density of birth dates is smooth at the cutoff (p = 0.71), predetermined characteristics balance across the cutoff (joint p = 0.58), and placebo cutoffs at 17 other birth dates produce estimates centered on zero.
The reform changes retirement behavior at once. The probability that a worker claims a pension by age 61 falls by 58.4 percentage points (s.e. 0.9) at the cutoff. Employment at ages 60 to 62 rises by 14.2 percentage points (s.e. 0.7) from a baseline of 52.1 percent, and annual earnings rise by 3,420 kora (s.e. 210) from a baseline of 12,900 kora. The employment gain equals 24 percent of the claims delayed by the reform. Disability and unemployment insurance absorb 52 percent of the delayed claims, and the remaining 24 percent of delayed claimants leave the labor force and live on household income.
The retained workers sit in specific firms, and we follow them there. We measure each firm's exposure as the share of its employees born in the 24 months after the cutoff, and we instrument the number of retained workers aged 60 to 62 with exposure interacted with calendar year. The instrument delivers the number of older workers a firm retains because of the reform, with a first-stage F-statistic of 412. Each older worker retained at a firm reduces the firm's hiring of workers under 30 by 0.31 (s.e. 0.07). Hiring of workers aged 30 to 49 moves by 0.02 (s.e. 0.06), and hiring of workers aged 50 and above moves by 0.01 (s.e. 0.04).
The estimates imply that the reform raises net employment by 6.9 persons for every 10 older workers retained. The displacement of 3.1 young hires per 10 retained workers offsets 31 percent of the older employment gain, and the economy keeps the remaining 69 percent as a net addition. The lump-of-labor prediction of a displacement ratio of 1 lies 9.9 standard errors above the estimate.
The adjustment runs through quantities. Entry wages of new hires change by 0.3 percent (s.e. 0.4), and wage scales in collective agreements hold constant, so headcount carries the full adjustment to the new age composition. Firms with deep promotion ladders and high shares of workers aged 60 to 62 account for 71 percent of the decline in youth hiring. In these firms, the share of workers aged 30 to 39 promoted within two years falls by 2.2 percentage points (s.e. 0.5) from a baseline of 11.4 percent. Retained older workers occupy rungs of the ladder and slow the vacancy chain that creates entry-level positions.
The decline in youth hiring carries a cost for the workers who enter the labor market after the reform. Workers aged 25 to 29 in 2018 through 2020 earn 1,570 kora (s.e. 380) less per year in their first two years of employment, and their cumulative loss over five years equals 5,900 kora (s.e. 1,350), or 41 percent of one year's median earnings at ages 25 to 29. The cost accumulates in the cohorts that enter employment when retained workers occupy the ladder.
A model of the firm organizes the results. The firm combines older and younger workers in production, hires at entry wages set by collective agreements, and maintains a promotion ladder with a fixed number of rungs. The model delivers a displacement ratio equal to the ratio of the cross-partial to the own-partial of the production function in young workers, and it predicts that displacement concentrates in firms with deep ladders. Section III derives the prediction, and Section VII confirms it in the data.
This paper contributes to three literatures. The first studies the labor supply response to pension rules. Havrick and Dellamore (2014) and Ostrander, Quillon, and Pellam (2019) estimate the response of older workers' employment to eligibility ages in Eastfold and on the Tessaly Coast, and report increases of 9 to 16 percentage points. We reproduce this response in the Marden data at 14.2 percentage points and extend the analysis to the employers of the retained workers.
The second literature tests the lump-of-labor hypothesis. Tesserine (2008) finds a displacement ratio of zero across 11 reforms with country-level data, and Varkesh and Oldmere (2017) find ratios of 0.5 to 1.0 with regional variation in northern Marden. Our firm-level instrument delivers a ratio of 0.31 with a standard error of 0.07, and the linked data identify the margin on which firms adjust. The estimate sits between the country-level and regional benchmarks and rests on 187,400 firms observed for 13 years.
The third literature studies the internal organization of firms and the allocation of positions along promotion ladders. Harbeck, Sorrelly, and Ketterby (2012) model promotion as a vacancy chain, and Marrowick (2020) documents slow turnover in the top rungs of the Karsten civil service. We estimate the effect of a shock to retirement ages on promotion and on hiring at the entry rung, and the estimates quantify the vacancy chain.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section I describes the Pension Age Act and the institutional setting. Section II presents the data. Section III lays out the conceptual framework. Section IV describes the empirical strategy. Section V reports the effects on older workers. Section VI reports the effects on firms and younger workers. Section VII studies mechanisms and heterogeneity. Section VIII concludes.
