VIII. Conclusion
This paper measures the displacement of younger workers by older workers retained through a rise in the pension eligibility age. The Pension Age Act of 2011 in Marden raises employment at ages 60 to 62 by 14.2 percentage points and reduces hiring of workers under 30 by 0.31 for each retained older worker. Net employment rises by 6.9 persons per 10 retained workers, and the lump-of-labor benchmark of one-for-one displacement lies 9.9 standard errors from the estimate. Entry wages and sales stay constant, and payroll rises by 3.8 percent, so firms adjust through headcount and promotion rates. Firms with deep promotion ladders and many older workers carry 71 percent of the displacement.
Three implications follow. First, pension age reforms raise aggregate employment. Projections that assume zero displacement overstate the employment gain by 45 percent, and the Marden estimate supplies the displacement ratio for the projections of the 14 member states of the Meridian Economic Council that raised their pension ages. Second, the cost of the reform falls on young entrants. Workers who enter employment in 2018 through 2020 lose 5,900 kora of earnings over five years, and the loss concentrates in sectors with deep ladders. Third, the vacancy chain links the retirement margin to the hiring margin. Any shock to retention, including mandatory retirement rules and changes in occupational plans, moves hiring through the ladder.
The framework applies to every setting with collective wage scales and promotion ladders. The displacement ratio equals the productivity of the retained worker relative to the entrant, and the ladder depth measures the number of positions that a retained worker blocks. These two primitives organize the effects of the reform on firms, workers, and public budgets in Marden and in the other states that extend working lives.
