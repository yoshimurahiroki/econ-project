# PLANT CLOSURES AND THE LOCAL LABOR MARKET: EVIDENCE FROM LINKED EMPLOYER-EMPLOYEE RECORDS

ANNA KESSLER, TOMÁS RIVAL, AND YUKI SATO

This article studies the effects of large plant closures on the local labor markets in which they occur. We focus on 214 closures of manufacturing plants with at least 500 employees in Velmora between 1995 and 2015, decided by multinational parents in response to global restructuring. Using the universe of employer-employee records and 3.1 million establishment-year observations, we estimate the effect of a closure on displaced workers, on workers at other employers in the same commuting zone, and on the plant's local suppliers. Closure timing was set by headquarters outside Velmora, and commuting zones with and without a closure follow the same employment trends before the announcement. We find that a closure reduces employment in the commuting zone by 1.6 jobs for every job lost at the plant, that the additional loss is concentrated at local suppliers, and that the employment of nondisplaced workers recovers within four years while the employment of displaced workers remains 9.2 percentage points lower after ten years. *JEL codes:* J63, J65, R23.

## I. INTRODUCTION

A growing literature in economics has documented the earnings losses of workers displaced by mass layoffs (Harlow and Penn 2009; Oduya 2016). It remains an open question how much of a plant's employment a local labor market loses when the plant closes. This article answers that question by studying the effect of large plant closures on employment at the plant, at its local suppliers and at other employers in the same commuting zone. Local governments subsidize the entry of large plants on the premise that each plant job supports additional local jobs. The size of the local multiplier on the way out is therefore a central input for the design of these subsidies.

Large manufacturing plants make up a particularly concentrated source of local employment in Velmora. Plants with at least 500 employees account for 31 percent of manufacturing employment, and in 62 of the country's 118 commuting zones a single plant employs more than 5 percent of the private workforce.

Between 1995 and 2015, multinational parents closed 214 such plants as part of global restructuring programs. Parent companies announced each closure between 9 and 14 months before production ended, and the decision followed the parent's global capacity plan.

This article analyzes the effect of plant closures by linking records on closure announcements (1995–2015) with the universe of employer-employee records (1990–2020) and the input-output transactions of all incorporated firms (1993–2020). We use a stacked difference-in-differences design, leveraging the fact that closure timing was set by headquarters outside Velmora. Each closure is compared with commuting zones that experience no closure within five years, matched on industry composition and employment growth in the five years before the announcement.

The setting is relevant for economic policy because Velmora's regional development agency spent 4.8 billion euros on plant-entry subsidies between 1995 and 2015, and its evaluation rule assumes a local multiplier of 1.5.

We estimate the influence of a closure on a range of outcomes. First, to measure the total local effect, we compute employment in the commuting zone relative to its pre-announcement level. We find that the commuting zone loses 1.6 jobs for every job lost at the plant five years after the closure. Second, we find that suppliers within the commuting zone lose 0.41 jobs for every job lost at the plant, and that this loss accounts for 68 percent of the additional decline. Third, at the worker level, we find that displaced workers remain 9.2 percentage points less likely to be employed ten years after the closure, while nondisplaced workers in the same commuting zone return to their pre-announcement employment rate within four years.

These results provide evidence on the old question of whether local multipliers are symmetric. A multiplier of 1.6 on the way out lies close to the evaluation rule's multiplier of 1.5 on the way in, and 68 percent of the additional loss runs through the plant's local supply chain. The persistent losses of displaced workers alongside the recovery of other workers locate the long-run cost of a closure in the careers of the workers it displaces.

The article proceeds as follows. Section II describes the closures and the data. Section III presents the empirical design. Section IV reports the effects on the commuting zone and on suppliers. Section V reports the effects on displaced and nondisplaced workers. Section VI concludes.
