Public Ratings with Heterogeneous Buyers
Every week, an online exchange for rental housing in Port Halvard assigns a quality grade to each of 2,300 listings. A listing with grade A attracts three times the inquiries of a listing with grade B, and the exchange chooses the cutoffs between the grades. Buyers carry individual quality requirements: a student inquires at a posterior quality of 0.4, and a family inquires at 0.8. The exchange earns a fee on every inquiry, and the grading rule determines how many buyers inquire.
This paper characterizes the grading rule that maximizes inquiries. A sender observes the quality of a listing, a binary state, and commits to a rating that maps the state into a distribution over grades. A continuum of buyers with privately known thresholds observes the grade, forms a posterior by Bayes' rule, and buys if the posterior reaches the threshold. The sender's payoff equals the mass of buyers who buy.
Theorem 1 shows that the sender's value equals the concave envelope of the threshold distribution evaluated at the prior. The optimal rating induces two posteriors. The lower posterior sits at the largest point at or below the prior where the envelope touches the distribution, and the upper posterior sits at the smallest point at or above the prior where it does. A rating with two grades, certified and uncertified, attains the value of any finer rating, so the coarse grading rule used by most platforms gives up zero value.
An example fixes ideas. Thresholds are uniform on [0.4, 0.8], and the prior probability of high quality equals 0.5. An uninformative rating sells to 25 percent of buyers, because the buyers with thresholds below 0.5 buy at the prior. Full disclosure sells to 50 percent. The optimal rating certifies the listing with probability 0.625 and sets the certified posterior at 0.8, the largest threshold in the population. Every buyer buys after certification, and the optimal rating sells to 62.5 percent of buyers.
The result reflects a trade between the mass of buyers served at a posterior and the probability of reaching it. Raising the certified posterior from a to b brings the buyers with thresholds in [a, b] into the market and lowers the probability of certification, because posteriors average to the prior. The optimal pair equates the density of the marginal buyer with the slope of the chord, which gives the tangency conditions in Theorem 1.
Proposition 2 characterizes three benchmarks. Full disclosure is optimal at every prior if and only if the threshold distribution lies below the 45-degree line. An uninformative rating is optimal at every prior if and only if the distribution is concave. The value of the optimal rating is concave and nondecreasing in the prior. The first benchmark covers threshold distributions that first-order stochastically dominate the uniform distribution on [0, 1], and the second covers distributions with a nonincreasing density.
Section IV applies the results to the rental exchange. The exchange publishes four grades. The optimal two-grade rating raises inquiries by 18 percent relative to the current rule, and the gain concentrates in listings with prior quality between 0.3 and 0.6.
The paper contributes to the literature on information design. Brevane and Tessaly (2011) and Oldmere (2015) solve for the optimal signal with a single receiver and show that the sender's value equals a concave envelope of the receiver-indirect payoff. We extend the envelope characterization to a continuum of receivers with private thresholds and show that the two-posterior structure carries over. Quillfeather, Rosterman, and Yarrowby (2018) study a public signal for two receivers and obtain the optimal rating by enumeration, and our tangency conditions give the solution for any number of receivers.
The paper also contributes to the study of ratings and certification in markets. Wexcombe (2013) shows that a monopoly certifier chooses a threshold grading rule when buyers share a common requirement. Thornlee and Ambrelle (2020) estimate the effect of grade cutoffs on listing demand in Karsten. Our result extends the threshold grading rule to buyers with heterogeneous requirements and locates the certified posterior at a tangency point of the requirement distribution.
The proofs rest on two facts: Bayes consistency of the posterior distribution, and the representation of a concave envelope as a chord between two points. Section I sets up the model. Section II proves Theorem 1. Section III states the benchmarks and the comparative statics. Section IV applies the results to the rental exchange, and Section V extends the model to a sender with a cost of precision. Section VI concludes.
