# Delayed Retirement and the Employment of Younger Workers

## Motivation

- 14 of the 20 Meridian Economic Council states raised the pension eligibility age between 2005 and 2022
- Official spending projections assume retained older workers add to total employment
- Lump of labor: each retained older worker takes the position of a younger worker

## Question

How many hires of younger workers does a firm give up for each older worker it retains?

- Pins down the net employment effect of the reform
- Pins down the incidence of the reform across age groups

## This paper

- Variation: Pension Age Act of 2011 in Marden raises the eligibility age from 60 to 63 for workers born on or after January 1, 1958
- Data: universe of social insurance records linked to the employer-employee panel, 2010–2022
- Design: birth-date cutoff for workers, firm exposure to the cutoff for employers

## Preview of findings

- Employment at ages 60–62 rises by 14.2 pp (s.e. 0.7)
- Each retained older worker reduces hiring of workers under 30 by 0.31 (s.e. 0.07)
- Net gain: 6.9 jobs for every 10 older workers retained
- Workers entering in 2018–2020 lose 5,900 kora over five years

## Setting

- Eligibility age 60 for cohorts born before January 1, 1958, and 63 for later cohorts
- A worker born on December 31, 1957 qualifies in 2017; a worker born one day later qualifies in 2021
- Cohorts on either side share the same labor market, firms and macroeconomic conditions

## Data

- Marden Social Insurance Agency: monthly employment spells, earnings, pension claims, employer
- Marden Revenue Service: annual payroll and hiring for 412,000 firms
- Sample: 452,310 workers born within 18 months of the cutoff; 187,400 firms with 10 or more employees

## Empirical strategy: workers

- $Y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + \beta_1 b_i + \beta_2 D_i b_i + \varepsilon_i$, $D_i = 1[b_i \geq 0]$
- Local linear, triangular kernel, bandwidth $h$ = 18 months
- Birth dates are fixed 51 years and 9 months before the Act
- Density of birth dates smooth at the cutoff (p = 0.71)
- Predetermined characteristics balance (joint p = 0.58)

## Empirical strategy: firms

$$
R_{jt} = \pi\,(E_j \times \mathrm{Post}_t) + X_{jt}'\gamma + \mu_j + \eta_t + v_{jt}
$$

$$
H^a_{jt} = \psi_a R_{jt} + X_{jt}'\delta_a + \mu_j + \eta_t + e^a_{jt}
$$

- $E_j$: share of 2010 employees born in the 24 months after the cutoff
- $R_{jt}$: retained workers per 100 employees; $H^a_{jt}$: hires in age group $a$
- First-stage $F$ = 412; standard errors clustered by firm

## Retirement and employment at ages 60–62

- Pension claims by age 61: −58.4 pp (s.e. 0.9)
- Employment at ages 60–62: +14.2 pp (s.e. 0.7) from a baseline of 52.1%
- Annual earnings: +3,420 kora (s.e. 210) from a baseline of 12,900 kora
- Delayed claims: 24% employment, 52% disability and unemployment insurance

## Displacement of younger workers

| Hires per retained older worker | Estimate | s.e. |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Workers under 30 | −0.31 | 0.07 |
| Workers aged 30–49 | 0.02 | 0.06 |
| Workers aged 50 and above | 0.01 | 0.04 |

- Lump-of-labor ratio of 1 lies 9.9 standard errors above the estimate

## Mechanism: the promotion ladder

- Entry wages: +0.3% (s.e. 0.4); collective wage scales unchanged
- Firms with deep ladders and high shares aged 60–62: 71% of the decline in youth hiring
- Promotion within two years at ages 30–39: −2.2 pp (s.e. 0.5) from 11.4%

## Cost to entering cohorts

- Workers aged 25–29 in 2018–2020
- First two years: −1,570 kora per year (s.e. 380)
- Five years: −5,900 kora (s.e. 1,350), 41% of one year's median earnings

## Bottom line

- Displacement ratio: 0.31 young hires per retained older worker
- The reform keeps 69% of the older employment gain as net employment
- Headcount at the entry rung carries the adjustment
