## Abstract

Commuting subsidies change the benefits and costs of employment policy through household participation and firms' hiring. We randomly assign subsidy levels across 48 labor markets in a fictional economy and evaluate employment and social surplus over eight weeks. A JPY 40,000 subsidy raises the employment rate by 9.19 percentage points; the 95% confidence interval is [5.75, 12.63]. Combining household surplus, firm profit, the external benefit of employment and fiscal costs gives an estimated surplus effect of JPY 1.68 thousand per household, with a 95% confidence interval of [-4.44, 7.80] thousand. Ranking the three subsidy levels by estimated surplus selects JPY 40,000.

## Introduction

A commuting subsidy raises the return households receive from taking a job. Firms expand hiring as labor supply increases, and wages adjust. Choosing the subsidy therefore requires a policy comparison that incorporates the responses of both households and firms.

We construct a fictional economy linking household participation to firms' hiring and compare commuting subsidies of JPY 0, 20,000 and 40,000. We randomly assign the subsidy at the market level and measure employment, household surplus, firm profit and fiscal spending for the same 4,800 households. The policy objective is social surplus over eight weeks. A common monetary valuation links each subsidy to the allocation it produces.

## Model and design

Each household compares its wage plus the subsidy, net of commuting costs, with the value of home production. Firms compare wages with a marginal product that declines as employment expands. The market wage equates household labor supply with firms' labor demand. The subsidy lowers reservation wages and changes employment and wages jointly.

Each market contains 100 households. We assign 16 of the 48 markets to each subsidy arm and measure employment and market wages over the same eight-week period. The estimands are the assignment effects of the subsidy levels. Estimation uses the market assignment probabilities to recover average effects across households.

## Results

The JPY 40,000 subsidy increases the employment rate by 9.19 percentage points relative to the zero-subsidy arm. The 95% confidence interval is [5.75, 12.63] percentage points. The estimated change in the market wage is JPY -28.79 thousand per worker. The expansion of labor supply and the wage adjustment support firms' increased hiring.

Households and firms both gain from this adjustment. The estimated household-surplus gain is JPY 5.61 thousand per household, with a standard error of 1.14 thousand. The firm-profit gain, expressed over the same household population, is JPY 14.88 thousand, with a standard error of 2.81 thousand. Fiscal spending increases by JPY 23.32 thousand per household.

The policy objective adds household surplus, firm profit and the external benefit of employment, then accounts for subsidy payments and financing costs. Valuing the external benefit at JPY 100,000 per job and the fiscal excess burden at 0.2 times spending gives a surplus effect of JPY 1.68 thousand per household. Its 95% confidence interval is [-4.44, 7.80] thousand. This valuation and the common household population determine the ranking of the three subsidies.

## Conclusion

The estimated-surplus rule selects a JPY 40,000 subsidy in this example. Its effects operate through household participation, wage adjustment and firms' hiring. Combining those responses with fiscal costs makes the subsidy choice a comparison of social surplus.
