## Abstract

Transport investment changes where people live and work and distributes its gains through wages and rents. We evaluate a link between the center and outer district of a fictional city with three districts and 12,000 workers, together with a 20% expansion of outer-district floor supply. The transport policy yields an annual net income-equivalent gain of JPY 1,768.830 million. Adding floor supply raises the worker income equivalent to JPY 2,837.550 million and changes owner income after the policy charge by JPY −285.665 million. Their sum, JPY 2,551.884 million, selects the combined transport and floor-supply policy.

## Introduction

A shorter journey between the center and outer district changes the residence-workplace combinations that workers value. Their location choices move local labor supply and housing demand. Firms adjust employment and commercial floor use to wages and rents. These responses distribute the gains from transport investment between workers and floor owners.

We compare transport and floor-supply policies for the same 12,000 workers in a three-district city. The authority has an annual resource budget of JPY 1,500 million. It chooses a policy by adding the worker income equivalent to the change in owners' income after policy costs. Evaluating access, floor supply and resource costs over a common population and year gives the comparison supporting that choice.

## Measurement and model

We construct district residents and jobs by summing disjoint subdistrict and workplace records. Annual lease revenue divided by total leased floor area gives rent per square meter. District land areas and nine directed residence-workplace travel times complete the shared tables. These measurements put population, prices, density and access on a common district basis.

Workers choose their residence and workplace using wages, rents, travel times and residential amenities. Firms combine labor and commercial floor space to produce the traded good. Their marginal products and factor prices determine employment and commercial floor demand. Residential and commercial demand together exhaust each district's floor supply. Employment and residential density affect productivity and amenities, feeding back into location choices.

At the adopted elasticities and commuting-cost coefficient, resident counts, jobs and rents recover baseline wages, productivity, amenities and floor supply. The baseline mean annual wage is JPY 4 million. We carry this equilibrium into a policy that reduces center–outer travel time from 40 to 20 minutes, then combine that link with a 20% expansion of outer-district floor supply.

## Policy comparison

Transport service costs JPY 300 million annually. The combined policy adds 66,640.067 square meters of floor services and has an annual cost of JPY 833.121 million. Floor owners finance the policy. Output funds worker consumption, owner consumption after the charge and the resources used by the policy.

Transport investment gives workers JPY 1,704.463 million of income equivalent and raises owners' net income by JPY 64.367 million. Adding floor supply gives workers JPY 2,837.550 million of income equivalent. Owners' rental receipts rise by JPY 547.455 million, and their income after the policy charge changes by JPY −285.665 million. The resulting net gain is JPY 2,551.884 million over the same population and year.

Changes in transport and floor supply move both the allocation of activity across districts and the allocation of income between workers and owners. Valuing worker gains at baseline prices and adding the change in owners' net income connects those adjustments to the policy objective.

## Conclusion

The annual net income-equivalent criterion selects the combined transport and floor-supply policy. Its cost of JPY 833.121 million and net gain of JPY 2,551.884 million provide the budget and benefit comparison. Connecting residence-workplace choice, floor-market adjustment and cost financing yields the citywide policy decision.
