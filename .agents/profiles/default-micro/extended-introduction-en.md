**Ideal output:**

Joint activation of linked production schedules reduces daily peak electricity demand by 1.3 megawatts more than private activation. In our randomized experiment across food-processing estates, daily peak demand averages 10 megawatts in the control group; private activation reduces it by 0.5 megawatts and joint activation by 1.8 megawatts. The 95 percent confidence interval for the additional reduction is 0.85 to 1.75 megawatts. Daily peak demand is the maximum of the plants' combined load at any one time. Plants that transfer food between them can change their production times together, preserving handoffs as they move electricity-intensive stages out of expensive hours. We test this implementation rule while holding electricity tariffs, the planning rule, equipment and agreed supply quantities fixed.

The production constraints define each estate's feasible schedules. Each estate has a common electricity feeder. Plants wash, cook or chill food in batches, and some transfer intermediate products to other plants within the estate. All plants face higher electricity prices from 7 a.m. to 10 a.m., when many batches begin. Movable batch starts have 30 to 90 minutes of timing flexibility. A feasible schedule preserves process order, staffing availability, product-holding limits and dispatch windows. A jointly feasible set of linked shifts preserves a handoff; a single plant's shift can break that handoff. The plant's choice is whether to shift production time while preserving compatibility with the other stages.

We randomly assign 90 estates containing 2,160 plants to three groups for 18 weeks after an eight-week baseline period. The control group retains the existing tariff and receives no scheduling recommendations. The other two groups receive recommendations from a common rule that minimizes the estate's highest aggregate 15-minute load over its feasible schedules. Both groups receive the same planning information and staff contact. Under private activation, each plant's acceptance activates its schedule independently of its partners' acceptance. Under joint activation, an interdependent set of changes takes effect when every plant required for it agrees. An incomplete set of agreements leaves those plants on their previous schedules. Plants choose whether to accept the recommendations in both groups. The joint-activation rule uses neither tariff discounts nor transfers between firms. Electricity loads and production decisions affect other plants on the same feeder, so we randomize whole estates. The analysis compares outcomes by assigned group and includes accepting and rejecting plants in their assigned groups.

Full-day meter readings place the load removed from the morning peak in adjacent, cheaper hours. Joint activation's estimated effect on total daily electricity use is 0.3 percent, with a confidence interval from −0.8 to 1.4 percent. Joint activation reduces electricity bills by 5.2 percent.

Implementation records distinguish plants by time-sensitive production links identified before the intervention. Among linked plants, 34 percent of recommended changes are carried out under private activation and 76 percent under joint activation. Among plants without those links, the shares are 79 and 81 percent. Firms in both groups receive information about the linked changes their own recommendations require. Joint activation makes each accepted change contingent on the other required changes taking effect together.

Delivery and output records measure how production timing affects firms' completion of orders. Private activation increases the share of within-estate deliveries missing their agreed windows by 3.6 percentage points from a control mean of 7 percent. Joint activation's estimated increase is 0.1 percentage point, with a confidence interval from −0.7 to 0.9 percentage points. Joint activation's estimated effect on saleable output valued at fixed pre-intervention product prices is 0.2 percent, with a confidence interval from −1.1 to 1.5 percent.

Previous research studies prices, production links and schedule feasibility. Studies of time-of-use prices find that isolated plants shift electricity-intensive work away from expensive hours (F1). Records from buyers with available machines link suppliers' delivery delays to subsequent production delays at buyers (F2). Engineering analyses of fully implemented schedules construct flatter load profiles that satisfy process and delivery constraints (F3). Our experiment holds prices and planning information fixed and varies the rule governing the implementation of mutually dependent changes. It tests voluntary implementation of technically feasible flexibility and measures effects on the shared peak, deliveries and output.
